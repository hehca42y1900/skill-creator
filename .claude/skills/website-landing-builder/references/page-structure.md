# Page Structure Reference

Detail on each block from SKILL.md's page architecture: what makes it work, and the common way it fails.

## Contents
- Nav
- Hero
- Problem / value proposition
- Features → benefits
- Social proof
- How it works
- Pricing
- FAQ
- Final CTA
- Footer

## Nav

Keep it out of the visitor's way. On a single-purpose landing page, a logo and one CTA button is often the whole nav — every extra link is an exit. On a multi-page marketing site, a fuller nav is appropriate, but the primary CTA should still be visually distinct from the rest of the items (filled button vs. text links).

**Common failure**: a nav with 6+ equal-weight links that competes with the hero CTA for attention, or a sticky nav that eats too much vertical space on mobile.

## Hero

The hero carries the whole pitch. It needs, in order of importance: a headline stating the specific outcome or transformation, a sub-headline that adds the one clarifying detail (for whom, how), and a primary CTA. A supporting visual — product screenshot, short demo, illustration — should reinforce the claim, not just decorate the page.

Pick the hero form deliberately rather than defaulting to "big number + small label + gradient": a live product screenshot works for tools people can picture using; a single powerful stat works for data-driven claims; an interactive demo works when the product's value is best shown, not told; a strong single sentence works when the claim itself is the differentiator.

**Common failure**: a headline that describes the category ("The future of project management") instead of the outcome ("Ship projects two weeks faster"). Category headlines could sit on any competitor's page — that's the tell that it needs sharpening.

## Problem / value proposition

State the situation the visitor is in before they arrive — the friction, cost, or risk they're currently living with — before pivoting to how the product changes it. This section earns the rest of the page the right to be read, because it shows the visitor you understand their situation specifically, not generically.

**Common failure**: skipping straight to "our solution" without ever naming the problem, which reads as generic even when the solution is genuinely good.

## Features → benefits

Every feature line should answer "so what?" A feature is what the product does; a benefit is what changes for the person using it. Pair them: state the feature briefly, then the outcome. Group related features into 3-4 thematic clusters rather than a flat list of 10+ items — visitors skim, they don't read spec sheets.

**Common failure**: a features grid that's just internal product terminology restated with icons, with no translation into visitor-facing outcomes.

## Social proof

Effective placement is distributed, not dumped in one testimonials block: a customer logo strip works well right after the hero (borrowed trust, before the visitor has invested any reading time), a specific testimonial or stat works well after the features section (reinforcing the claim just made), and a case study or detailed quote works well before pricing (removing doubt right before the ask).

Specificity beats volume. One testimonial with a name, role, company, and a concrete result ("cut onboarding from 3 weeks to 4 days") outweighs five generic "Great product!" quotes.

**Common failure**: stock-photo avatars with vague quotes that could apply to any product — this reads as fabricated even when it isn't, and erodes trust rather than building it.

## How it works

Use this section when there's real onboarding friction to demystify, or when the mechanism itself is a selling point (e.g., "no code required" is more credible when you show the 3 steps). Keep it to 3-5 steps; more than that undercuts the "this is easy" message you're trying to send.

**Common failure**: including this section by default even when the product is self-explanatory, padding the page without adding persuasion.

## Pricing

Simple and anchored beats clever. If there are multiple tiers, visually distinguish the recommended one. State what's included in plain terms — avoid vague tier names without substance ("Pro" with no explanation of what "Pro" unlocks). Hidden pricing ("Contact us") should be a deliberate choice for genuinely custom/enterprise sales, not a default to avoid the conversation.

**Common failure**: burying the actual price behind a click when the visitor's next action depends on knowing it.

## FAQ

This section's job is objection handling, not general documentation. Write the questions a skeptical visitor is actually holding back — about cost, commitment, data, switching cost, support — not questions that flatter the product. If you don't know the real objections, ask, or infer them from the pricing model and the target audience's likely hesitations.

**Common failure**: generic filler questions ("What is [Product]?") that don't address any real hesitation, taking up space that could hold the questions that actually block conversion.

## Final CTA

Restate the offer once more, ideally with a slightly different angle than the hero (e.g., hero = the outcome, final CTA = urgency or a risk-reversal like a free trial/guarantee). Don't make the final CTA the *only* other place to convert — repeat the primary CTA at natural pauses throughout the page, not just top and bottom.

## Footer

This is where trust and legal signals live (company info, privacy/terms, contact, social links) — keep it visually quiet and out of competition with the primary conversion path above it.
