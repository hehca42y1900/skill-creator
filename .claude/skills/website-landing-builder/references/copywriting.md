# Copywriting Reference

Words on a landing page are conversion tools, not decoration. Every sentence should either remove a doubt or move the visitor toward the CTA — if it does neither, cut it.

## Headline formulas

Reach for these before defaulting to a category tagline. Pick the one that fits what's actually true about the product — don't force a formula onto a claim it doesn't support.

- **Outcome-first**: "[Specific result] in [timeframe/effort]" — e.g. "Cut invoice time from 2 hours to 10 minutes."
- **Audience + transformation**: "[Product] helps [specific audience] [do specific thing]" — e.g. "Helps freelance designers get paid on time, every time."
- **Problem-agitation**: name the pain directly — e.g. "Stop losing candidates to slow hiring."
- **Mechanism as differentiator**: when *how* it works is the selling point — e.g. "The only CRM that updates itself from your inbox."

Test any headline against this: could a direct competitor put this exact sentence on their page? If yes, it's not specific enough yet.

## Sub-headlines

The sub-headline's job is to answer the one question the headline raises — usually "for whom," "how," or "at what cost." Keep it to one sentence. It should feel like the headline's necessary second half, not a restatement.

## CTA button copy

Prefer a verb + concrete outcome over a generic verb alone:

| Weak | Stronger |
|---|---|
| Submit | Get my free quote |
| Learn more | See how it works |
| Sign up | Start free — no card needed |
| Download | Get the guide |

Keep the button label consistent with what happens next — a button that says "Start free trial" should not lead to a page asking for a credit card first without warning; mismatched expectations at the moment of conversion are one of the most common trust breaks.

## Feature-to-benefit translation

Pattern: **[Feature]** so that **[benefit]**, or lead with the benefit and let the feature justify it. Don't state the feature and stop — that leaves the "so what" to the visitor, and most won't do that work themselves.

Example: "Automatic backups every 15 minutes" (feature only) → "Automatic backups every 15 minutes, so a bad deploy never costs you more than a few minutes of work" (benefit surfaced).

## Objection-handling for FAQ / trust copy

Write from the actual hesitation, not a flattering rephrase of it:

- Cost hesitation: address total cost of ownership, not just sticker price — setup time, switching cost, what happens if they cancel.
- Trust hesitation: be specific about data handling, security, or track record — vague reassurance ("we take security seriously") reads as filler.
- Commitment hesitation: state the actual cancellation/refund terms plainly, don't bury them.

## Microcopy: forms, errors, empty states

- **Form labels** name what the person is entering ("Work email"), not implementation details.
- **Errors** state what went wrong and how to fix it, in a neutral tone — no apologizing, no vague "Something went wrong" when the actual cause (invalid email format, required field) is knowable.
- **Success states** confirm the action in the same words used on the button/label that triggered it, so the vocabulary stays consistent through the whole flow (button said "Join waitlist" → confirmation says "You're on the waitlist").
- **Empty states** (e.g. no results, no items yet) should suggest the next action, not just state absence.

## Tone

Match the register to the audience — a B2B security tool and a consumer wellness app should not read the same. Whatever the register, keep sentences short, use active voice ("We built X" not "X was built"), and cut qualifiers and filler ("really," "very," "simply," "just") that soften a claim without adding information.
